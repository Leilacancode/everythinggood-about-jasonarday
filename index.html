Everything good about Jason Arday

We are tired of the single reductionist narrative about the kindest person we knew. So we're gathering everything good about Jason Arday, in the words of the people who were there.

In memory of Jason Arday, 1985 - 2026

This site is built by Professor Jason Arday's former students.

We're collecting first-hand testimonies from people who knew him, learned from him, or crossed paths with him. Supervisions and seminars, emails, a reading list, a word of advice that stuck. Small things count. Small things are usually the whole point.

This isn't a place to debate the events of recent months, and we won't publish anything that is disrespectful to him and his family. Every submission here is read by one of us before it appears.

Testimonies

Jason stopped me after class to tell me I should become an academic. At that time, I was rejected from a PhD application, I wasn't sure if I should keep trying

He told me "it's their loss" and was willing to supervise me himself just to ensure I don't give up.

I'm doing my PhD elsewhere now. I always assumed I'd run into him at a conference one day and get to tell him. I'm so sad he isn't here to witness my growth, my journey is a trajectory he set off, I hope I'd have made him proud.

I met Jason in my first year as a grad student at Cambridge, when he visited the Faculty before becoming Professor. At the time I felt exhausted as one of the few black students at the Faculty and felt pretty isolated. When I saw him in the Faculty his smile was like a warm light in a cold winter. We had a chat about Blackness and Cambridge and I remember him telling me how important it is for us as minority academics to stick together. It was a short interaction but one that was significant - so reassuring to see a face like mine, especially one that was so kind and confident in this space. He really gave me hope that I could make it here too.

Thank you Jason.

Anon
PhD Student

I first met Jason when he joined the faculty. As a Professor of Sociology, his work directly intersected with my own research in sociology and higher education. Over several conversations, we spoke about the complexities of institutional change, what makes it difficult, and what truly makes it work. He always showed genuine appreciation for my involvement with the Faculty of Education Research Student Association (FERSA), believing deeply in the role student leadership plays in creating sustainable change. In a large academic institution, he made me feel seen.

During my term as FERSA President from 2023 to 2024, Jason was selected as the keynote speaker for our annual conference. Watching him speak to our community was inspiring, but what stayed with me most was what he shared afterward. He told me that he saw my potential in the field because I truly cared. As a person of color navigating the field of sociology, seeing his leadership and hearing his belief in me gave me the confidence to believe that I have a chance in academia, regardless of the social constraints.

Jason once shared two insights that I will carry with me forever. He said that sociology is a discipline enabled by love and community. He also spoke about his commitment to teaching, telling me that he strived to give his absolute best to other people’s children in the classroom, hoping that others would do the same for his own children one day .

I am heartbroken to learn of his passing so soon after my graduation. I never had the opportunity to reach out as an alumna to share where my journey has led, but I will forever remember Jason as a remarkable scholar, a devoted teacher, and a compassionate mentor.

Thank you, Jason, for your unwavering kindness, your belief in us, and your tireless efforts to create real change. You will be deeply missed.

I had the pleasure and the privilege of working with Jason at the University of Glasgow, where he very quickly became my friend. I am heartbroken about the death of this lovely, gentle, and kind man. The world has lost someone truly special.

Thank you for setting up this space for Jason. And thank you, Jason, for everything.

I was unsure about how to approach my dissertation which was on a challenging topic (grief education). Specifically, I couldn’t decide whether it was professional to include a personal narrative to preface the introduction. I stayed behind after class and asked Jason his thoughts. I’m fairly certain his advice to include it was what ultimately secured me a distinction for my dissertation (graded by two other lecturers), because this element was explicitly commented on positively. I’m grateful to Jason for being such an attentive and passionate lecturer. Students don’t always feel seen at Cambridge and many other lecturers had a distaste for teaching us, but he was talented teacher and I felt uniquely seen and understood by him.

Anonymous
PhD Student

I started working with Jason in 2013, at St Mary's University, at the point I knew of him and we'd had a few brief interactions, but nothing of note.

Not long after Jason accepted a post in the Physical accepted a role in my department we spoke on the phone about his excitement to be moving back down to London. I'll never forget, what was initially supposed to be a quick call ended up lasting nearly two hours.

Following the phone call Jason started working in the same department in September, working with him fundamentally changed the way I approach both lecturing and interacting with people broadly.

Having previously been employed as a graduate TA, I was always uncomfortable about being a similar age to the students and would often keep my head down on the walk from the station to campus dreading awkward conversations with students.

That was until I kept bumoing into Jason at the station, on the way between the station and campus he would talk to every one of our students he saw asking questiins about them and taking the time to get to know their interests.

As the year went on I saw him do so many small things thar made such a difference to student's experiences:

1)He'd greet them all by name when entering the classroom, often accompanying this with either a compliment or a question that related directly to them, e.g. how was the drive from Hillingdon? Showing them he remembered information about them.

2) His tutorials would last as long as a student needed to grasp a concept or understand how to improve their work often at his own expense - meaning he'd have to work late into the night on lecture prep or his own research.

3) He would stay and answer questions to students long after lectures finished - some times staying up to half an hour. Some times students would just stick around to show him social media videos about "aunty" and "uncle".

4) He would share his own personal stories with students but only once he knew their's too. He would use these stories to help students understand concepts

5) Working late into the night he would prioritise responding to students emails before starting his own work.

Over this time I was particularly priveleged to become friends with Jason. Spending hours with him, Stuart Whigham, Emma Newman and Amy Price planning lectures, seminars and putting the world to rights.

One moment that particular stood out to me was after the tragic passing of one of our first year students. The funeral was being held in South London in winter on a Saturday and it was freezing. Jason and I decided to meet at Clapham Junction and make our way to the funeral from there. Being cold I bought two hot chocolates and gave one to Jason when he arrived, to this day I've never met someone so excited or thankful for a hot chocolate.

When he left in 2014 to take a post at Leeds Beckett, he bought a number of colleagues personalised gift vouchers despite being the one who was leaving.

After Jason left St Mary's we stayed in touch at times talking nearly daily for many years, either calling each other or leaving voice notes with the content varying with anything from sociology, DEI initiatives, to professional wrestling, or music.

We'd often discuss things like whether he prefered The Jam or Liam Gallaghers version of Carnation, Jason always picked anything Liam did.

When comissioned to write his first edited collection - which was full of well known academics, he went out of his way to invite Stuart and I to write a chapter, something I'd never done before. Our first draft was week, and nearly got cut from the collection. However, the constructive and kind feedback provided by Heidi Mirza and Jason meant we were able to salvage the chapter and gave me my first publication as a lead author. Something, that built my confidence immeasurably. A chapter that was some what surprisingly turned into an article for the Guardian.

At every twist and turn I've faced in HE, Jason has been there to help me along the way and offer support. In truth the examples above don't even start to do justice to the impact he's had on me, whether it's the networks he supported me in building, the books and podcasts he exposed me too, or the time he took to listen to my ignorance and never judged me. Or the confidence he gave me to get involved with my university's Race Equity Network, and set up initiatives with my colleagues. He had a very gentle way of letting me know if my latest "bright idea" was tokenistic or misguided, without making me feel stupid or demoralised.

I can't believe I'll never receive a new voice note that starts "Hey Hobs how you doing?" or start a phone call with the same 10minutes of conversation before we move on to talk about what's new.

The crazy thing is I know so many others will have similar stories and while I feel incredibly priveleged to have called him a friend hundreds of people will have similar stories.

Professor Jason Arday, you are forever loved and forever missed.

Professor Jason Arday was one of the humblest, most caring and hardworking individuals I have ever known.

Not only was I able to call you my College tutor and teacher (Richmond College), and later my University lecturer (St. Mary’s), but I was fortunate enough to call you my friend.

I still remember our first day at Richmond College in 2009. We were waiting for enrolment when we were greeted by you, wearing chino shorts and boat shoes, and your massive smile. One of the ice breaker activities you selected was based on trust, whereby you stand behind someone, the person falls and the partner catches them. At first no one put their hand up to demonstrate, and I am pleased that I then built the confidence to step up. First I caught you (thank you for trusting in me), and then I turned around and entrusted you. As I fell, my doubts grew as I fell closer to the ground, but just before the ground you caught me, and from then on I knew you had our backs.

Your passion for education was second to none and could never be questioned. Soon after we started at Richmond, you met with the parents of our class, and I know you provided them with the warmest and most inspiring vision for their children to succeed.

Knowing your experiences of growing up and the challenges you faced, I know this epitomised your ethos within teaching. Any question, any interaction, no matter who it was, you always had time for people. No matter how big or small the issue, you were always willing to support.

You made it your mission to know everyone’s name within the College / University, whether they were a teacher or lecturer, or a member of the non-teaching staff. Your energy, motivation and passion were instilled within us all and enabled us to thrive in our studies and beyond.

You enabled us to unlock our potential, even when some of us couldn’t fully see what we were capable of ourselves.

Even after we had finished at College, you were always there to support me, have a conversation, or catch up about how things were going. You continued to inspire me to believe in my own potential, expressing I would make a great Headteacher, even though I couldn’t see it. I unfortunately didn’t get the chance to update you, but I have taken my next step on my journey and become Associate Senior Leader (Pastoral). Thank you for always believing in me.

I know you inspired thousands of individuals throughout your time lecturing, as well as countless others whose paths you crossed in wider life. You impacted people positively, just as you did me.

Your dedication to helping others didn’t stop at the classroom. From your fundraising initiatives, to the time you dedicated to homeless shelters, you were always looking for ways to make a difference.

And then there was your marathon running. I know I only joined you for a small part of one of those runs, from Twickenham to Teddington and back at around 5am, but your resilience and commitment were incredible. Completing 30 marathons in 35 days took a truly remarkable level of determination.

That is just one example of the impact you have had on my life, and I will support maintaining your legacy to inspire others.

You were an incredible individual, an incredible teacher, an incredible mentor, and an incredible friend.

I feel incredibly fortunate that our paths crossed.
